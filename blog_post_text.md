What's the biggest status symbol for the 1%, one that distinguishes them from the merely affluent in the rest of the top 10%? Is it the fancy extra home, or maybe a private jet? Here's an interesting prospect: what if it's having a third kid?

We just got the new Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF) for 2025 last week. This is a big Federal Reserve survey that comes out every three years, with the last being 2022. A useful and distinguishing thing about the SCF is that the Federal Reserve works really hard to get the top 1% well represented, which is hard to do. On top of the regular sample, they draw a sample of wealthy families from IRS records. It's a pain to do this, but it's essential if you want to understand how the economy works in terms of the sheer inequality of who owns what. It's a way we know, for instance, that the wealthiest 10% of families own 81% of total stocks while the bottom 50% only own 1%.

So let's get to kids. Measuring this is going to be tough because, while we get a great look at income and wealth, the Fed's summary data only counts the children in the household. But by digging into the full survey data, we can build a workable family child count. Let's take that, as well as focusing on mid-life households, with a reference person between 45 and 64. To get enough people in the 1%, I pool the last five surveys, going back to 2013. [All this analysis is here on Github for your AI to replicate](https://github.com/mtkonczal/blogs_2026_10_three_kids_SCF).

For kids, I combine the SCF's household child count with its count of living sons and daughters of either partner who live elsewhere ("how many sons and daughters do you (or your spouse) have who do not live with you?"). This includes stepchildren, and children-in-law who live in the household, so it is an imperfect family count, not a direct measure of births. Some children away from home, like college students, may also be counted twice, but checks that allow for this leave the positive gap we’ll find intact. Stepchildren raise a different worry about blended families, which I check at the end.

Now, the generic, textbook view is that with more income, you have fewer kids. That's what we've seen historically across countries. However, there's a “new era” of economics and fertility research that finds, across rich countries, "the income-fertility relationship has flattened and in some cases reversed, and the cross-country relationship between women's labor force participation and fertility is now positive” [(Doepke et al, 2023)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29948). People have been writing about this for some time. Economists are increasingly finding a “u-shaped” figure in education and incomes for fertility, with many proposing that buying child care cheaply relative to their incomes helps explain it [(Bar et. Al, 2018)](https://doi.org/10.20955/wp.2018.022). The child and family policy expert Elliot Haspel [wrote in 2019](https://www.romper.com/life/when-people-cant-afford-another-child-we-know-big-families-are-only-for-the-rich-15649230) about that when child care costs as much as it does, big families become something only the rich can afford. He finds a remarkable quote from a [2014 *New York Times* article](https://www.nytimes.com/2014/04/10/fashion/The-Growing-Three-Child-Household-in-Manhattan.html) where a New Yorker father says "At some level, the third child is a proxy for having enough wealth to have a very comfortable life."

Much of this work uses Census data like the American Community Survey; I want to try to extend it to the SCF here. So let's do a graph where the x-axis is all mid-life families, grouped by usual income (including capital income) relative to others their age, and the y-axis is the percent of families with a child count of at least three. Across much of the income scale it's pretty flat, around 40 to 43%, and it dips to 37% at the 80th–90th percentiles. But it has a statistically significant rise at the top 1%: 49%, compared with about 40% for the rest of the top 10%.

![Families with 3+ children by income](graphics/02_three_plus_kids_by_income.png)

Under the hood, this is driven by those with exactly three kids: about 34% of the top 1%, versus 24% of the rest of the top 10%. Four or more kids are about equally common in those two groups. So the third kid really is that sign you made it!

Is this about a third kid becoming a new cultural symbol for the 1%? Some digging makes me think this isn’t the case and the story might be more fascinating. A third kid isn’t new for them; the difference is new for everyone else. Compared to the 1990s, families with three or more kids became less common overall, except in the 1%, where the share was roughly unchanged, though with substantial uncertainty. Maybe all the stresses that made childbearing more difficult over the past 30 years just bounced right off the 1%? Here’s a quick glance at that:

![Families with 3+ children by income and period](graphics/04_three_plus_kids_by_period.png)

The top-1% gap is robust to many different ways of defining the 1%.\[^1\] Also, because it's the SCF, we can move from income to net worth as well. So here are families ranked by net worth, with approximate dollar labels. The share with three or more kids falls through much of the wealth distribution, from 47% around the 20th–30th percentiles down to 34% around the 70th–80th. Then, at the top 1%, it rises to 50%, compared with about 37% for the rest of the top 10%. So the same pattern shows up when we rank by wealth, and here it's not just the third kid: the top 1% are also more likely to have four or more (19% versus 14%).

![Families with 3+ children by net worth](graphics/05_three_plus_kids_by_networth.png)

Average number of kids by income increases for the 1%, but not in a statistically significant way; the average is noisier, since a few very large families move it a lot. However, ranked by net worth, the top 1% do have a significantly higher average (2.5 versus 2.25 kids), as you can see here:

![Average number of children by net worth](graphics/05b_mean_kids_by_networth.png)

We did note that remarriages among the 1% could be complicating this question, given the wording of the question about children living elsewhere. But excluding couples where either partner has been married more than once leaves a positive, statistically significant gap for both income and wealth in the five-survey pool. That doesn't rule out every kind of blended family, but remarriage doesn't explain away the result.

This is a correlation, but it's the one you'd expect if money solves the hard parts of raising kids. When you're in the 1%, a lot of the practical difficulties of childcare, of smoothing your income to get through the difficult few years, are probably not as big a problem. I don't think we can make everyone the 1%, but some of the things their money gives them like stability and support are things we could provide for young families right now. Why aren't we doing it?

\[^1\]: The charts rank families against others whose head is in the same five-year age band (45–49 through 60–64) in the same survey year. But the gap between the top 1% and the rest of the top 10% stays positive and statistically significant, for both income and wealth, if we instead use ten-year age bands, rank all families with a head aged 45 to 64 together, rank against the whole U.S. population, pool all five surveys into one ranking, or use actual rather than usual income. It also holds comparing the top 2% with the 90th–98th percentiles, and the top 0.5% with the 90th–99.5th. Across these, the gap runs from 7 to 15 points by income and 9 to 16 points by wealth.
