Executive summary

I found no factual or numerical claim in the post that is wrong as written. Every headline number reproduces from the raw Survey of Consumer Finances files, usually to the third decimal, and both footnotes’ robustness claims reproduce under the specification the methods document describes. The remaining issues are wording and disclosure, not arithmetic. The methodology audit, the independent reconstruction, the three weakest points and five easiest rebuttals follow in that order.

What checks out. The 2025 SCF release and design; the 81%/1% stock sentence (81.46% and 1.14% of EQUITY by net worth); the child-count construction and its stated defects; the Doepke, Bar, Hazan–Zoabi, Haspel and New York Times citations; the income and wealth curves; the exactly-three decomposition; the historical levels; footnote 1’s 15 alternative definitions; and footnote 2’s seven counting specifications, including its statement that families remain ranked against the full age-eligible sample. The independent reconstruction of the 12 headline statistics matches production to 10−6 on point estimates and to better than 0.001 on standard errors.

Small wording issues, not factual errors. “Compared with the 1990s” describes a period that begins in 1989; “four or more are about equally common” has a 95% interval of −5.83 to 4.31, so “not statistically distinguishable” is safer; footnote 1’s actual-income alternative is income-only; the methods’ “three families” X5910 statement is true in the 45–64 sample (two in 1989, one in 1992), not the full files (40 and 50 records, 8 and 10 families, above 10); and the 81% stock figure is EQUITY by net worth, not direct stock (90.87% for DEQ). None changes a number in the post.

A note on scope: “true” below means true as written in the reviewed draft, not that the underlying quantity is a birth history or a causal effect. The SCF measures a current-family child count at ages 45–64; it does not observe births, intentions, childcare prices or status. The post says this in the methods and, more briefly, in the text.

Methodology audit

I rebuilt the main analysis from the raw files rather than reading the production objects: 13 summary CSVs, 13 full Stata files, and 9 replicate-weight files. I used the saved production objects only for the published comparison and one period-change diagnostic. The rebuild reproduces the production sample, child counts, ranks, headline contrasts, standard errors and all 24 plotted bin values. I also ran checks the production scripts do not run: population-weighted waves, average ranks for ties, a re-ranked first-marriage subsample, an adult-roster adverse count, and a capped mean.

Data construction and joins

The raw archives were already present; I did not re-download them. This verifies the local October 2026 vintage, not a fresh network pull. The manifest records the archive MD5s and sizes.

The 13 waves are clean one-to-one joins. In every wave, the summary extract and the full public file have the same number of rows, Y1 is unique in both, every family appears in all five implicates, and the implicate index Y1 − 10×YY1 takes values 1–5 with equal rows per implicate. The summary weight identity holds record by record: the maximum relative error in 5×WGT = X42001 is 1.4×10−10 in 1989 and about 5×10−11 in every later wave. Reconstructing KIDS from the roster relationship codes gives zero disagreements in all 13 waves.

The 2013–2025 analysis sample is 11,215 families and 56,075 family–implicate rows. The top 1% by income contains 1,390 distinct year–family cases (12.4% of the unweighted sample, reflecting the oversample), and 208 in the 2025 wave alone. In any one wave and implicate, the top-1% bin holds 1.01% of the weight, the 90th–99th holds about 9.0%, and the 40th–60th holds about 20.0%, which is the check that the weighted ranks are doing what they claim.

The production merge in R/07_kids_income.R stops if the joined row count differs from the extract. That is sufficient for these files because (year, Y1) is unique in both; it is not a general key check. I verified uniqueness and exact ID-set equality independently. Adding stopifnot(!anyDuplicated(...)) would make the check self-documenting for a future vintage.

Dollar units are handled correctly: the summary extract is in 2025 dollars, the full public data are nominal, and every post comparison uses the extract. The only value-level edit in R/02 is blanking the usual-income categories in 1989 and 1992, where NORMINC was not collected; the analysis does not use those categories.

The child count

The formula is kids = KIDS + max(X5910, 0). KIDS counts roster relationship codes 4 (child, including in-law), 13 and 36 (foster child, suppressed in the public file). There is no age restriction, so adult children living at home count, and there are at most ten roster slots. X5910 is the “children living elsewhere” question; the 2025 wording is exactly what the post quotes, −1 means none, and there are no missing values or values below −1 in any wave.

The public files are top-coded at 10 from 1995 onward. In 1989 the full file reaches 16 and in 1992 it reaches 15. The methods says “three families in the 1989 and 1992 files report more.” That is true only in the 45–64 analysis sample: two families in 1989 and one in 1992. The full public files contain 40 families above 10 in 1989 and 50 in 1992. The sentence should say “in the age-eligible sample.” The practical effect is small: removing the cap changes the early 45–64 mean away-count by 0.006 children in 1989 and 0.0007 in 1992, and cannot change a 3+ indicator.

The double-count correction is implemented correctly. The roster-away count pairs relationship items X108…X226 with residence items X112…X230, persons three through twelve; a child coded 4/13/36 with residence 5 is a candidate for being reported again in X5910. Subtracting min(A, B) is the largest overlap arithmetically possible under that flag. The strict first-marriage restriction correctly requires both current-marital responses to be “married” (X8023 = 1, X105 = 1) and both first-marriage responses to be 1 (X7377 = 1, X7392 = 1), which excludes cohabiters. The older-sample check uses X19; substituting X104 gives identical results in this sample.

Two limitations the post states are real and cannot be fixed with these data: KIDS can include resident children-in-law and adult children, and X5910 includes stepchildren and children from previous marriages. The count is a current-family count, not a birth history. There are also missing final-slot roster fields in 1995–2004: only nine of the ten relationship slots exist, and the methods’ “all ten roster positions” is therefore slightly imprecise for those waves. The summary KIDS still matches the available slots exactly in every wave.

Sample, ranks and weights

The sample is the reference person aged 45–64. In a mixed-sex couple the reference person is the man, so a younger partner may still be fecund; the older-sample check addresses that. Ranks are weighted mid-ranks, (cumulative weight − own weight/2) / total weight, computed within survey year, implicate and five-year age band. The bin edges are right-closed, so the top 1% is rank > 0.99, consistently in R/07, R/25, R/28 and R/29. The 12 bins are the nine lower deciles plus 90–95, 95–99 and top 1%.

Each wave gets equal total weight (WGT / sum(WGT) within year and implicate). I tested the alternative: weighting waves by their population. The income gap moves from 9.14 to 9.08 points and the wealth gap from 12.94 to 13.01. The choice is immaterial here, but the post does not say which it uses; the methods and chart captions do.

Equal financial values are split by record order in the production rank. I tested the alternative of assigning the average rank to tied values. For wealth, where zero and negative net worth create large ties, the largest change in any bin’s 3+ share is under 0.06 percentage points, and the top-1% result is unchanged. Tie handling is not a source of the result.

Income is NORMINC, the Fed’s usual-income measure, which includes capital income and adds pension-account withdrawals. In 1989 and 1992 the usual-income amount was not collected, so those waves use actual INCOME; the historical section discloses this and runs an all-actual-income sensitivity. There are no missing NORMINC values in 1995–2025. Neither income nor net worth is equivalized for family size; the post compares families at the same age and rank, not families with the same per-person resources. That is a real limitation of the “money solves the hard parts” reading, and the methods states it.

Inference

The standard-error recipe is the Fed’s: 999 replicate weights for implicate 1, effective weight wt1b{r} × mm{r} with missing products set to zero, sampling variance with denominator 998, and imputation variance (1 + 1/5)B added. This matches the codebook’s MEANIT and MISECOMP formulas. Ranks are re-estimated inside every replicate, so the uncertainty in who is in the top 1% is carried through; that raises the income 3+ gap SE from about 3.55 to 3.75 and the wealth gap SE from about 3.61 to 3.80. The five waves are treated as independent cross-sections and their replicate variances are summed through the gradient of the pooled ratio; the top group and its comparison group are therefore allowed to covary.

I rebuilt the headline statistics with an independently written estimator and the same Fed inputs. The point estimates match production to 10−6; the standard errors match to better than 0.001 (income gap 3.7531 both; wealth gap 3.8007 both). The 24 charted bin values also reproduce. Two implementation details are worth recording. First, the production gradient is evaluated at implicate 1’s pooled moments rather than the five-implicate mean; using the mean changes the income gap SE from 3.7531 to 3.7545, which is immaterial. Second, the period-change test computes imputation variance on the paired recent-minus-early contrast; using the sum of the two periods’ imputation variances instead gives a slightly larger SE (about 0.05 points on the gap change), not a different conclusion.

The honest limitation is the one the methods states: the within-imputation sampling variance comes from implicate 1 alone because the Fed publishes replicate weights only for implicate 1. That is the standard SCF compromise, not a choice specific to this project. The intervals are pointwise normal approximations and are not adjusted for the number of outcomes and sensitivity definitions. The income headline p = 0.015 would not survive a strict correction across all the outcomes examined; the wealth headline p = 0.0007 is much more secure. That is another reason the wealth result deserves to lead.

Documentation precision

These are not numerical errors, but they are the places a careful reader will stumble.

  • “Three families” in the methods. True in the 45–64 sample (two in 1989, one in 1992); false for the full public files (40 and 50 family–implicate records, 8 and 10 families, above 10). Say “in the age-eligible sample.”
  • “Compared with the 1990s.” The early period is 1989–1998. Use the figure’s own label.
  • “Four or more are about equally common.” The gap is −0.76 points with a 95% interval of −5.83 to 4.31. “Not statistically distinguishable” is more precise.
  • Footnote 1’s actual-income alternative. It is an income-only check; the sentence says “for both income and wealth.”
  • The 81% stock sentence. It is EQUITY by net worth. On direct equity (DEQ) the top-10% share is 90.87%; on direct stocks it is 91.30%. The post’s “stock held by families” is defensible for the broad measure, but the methods should name the trusts/annuities/managed-account component.
  • The first-marriage estimand. The footnote says families remain ranked against the full age-eligible sample. That is the right disclosure: the 10.65-point wealth gap is the fixed-rank estimand, while the 4.17 re-ranked result is a different question (the top 1% of first-marriage couples), not a contradiction.
  • Equal wave weighting. The post says it pools the last five surveys; it does not say each wave gets equal total weight. The methods and captions do. One clause in the post would close it.
  • Implicate-1 counts. The 1,322 income-top families and 198–313 per wave are implicate-1 counts; the 1,390 is the number of distinct year–family cases entering the top group in at least one implicate. Label the denominator when quoting either.
  • “All ten roster positions.” 1995–2004 have nine relationship slots in the public file. The reconstruction still matches KIDS exactly.

Three weakest points

None of these is an arithmetic error. They are the places where the evidence is thinner than the prose, or where the post leaves out a choice that changes how a number should be read.

  1. The income “third kid” story weakens when you condition on already having two children. The headline compares the 3+ share among all families. That mixes the decision to have any children with the decision to have a third, and the top 1% differs on both margins. Among families with at least two children, the income top 1% has a 3+ share of 57.59% against 51.02% in the rest of the top 10%: a gap of 6.57 points, SE 4.07, 95% CI −1.41 to 14.55, p = 0.106. The wealth version survives: 60.46% versus 48.94%, gap 11.52 points, 95% CI 4.04 to 18.99. The descriptive 49-versus-40 comparison is correct, and the exactly-three decomposition is correct; what does not follow is that the income top 1% is specifically more likely to have a third child rather than more likely to have children at all. This is the single strongest reason to lead with wealth rather than income.

  2. The historical paragraph is a non-detection shown on an uncertainty-free chart. The top-1% share goes from 48.51% in 1989–1998 to 49.13% in 2013–2025: a change of +0.62 points with a standard error of 5.26 and a 95% interval of −9.69 to +10.93. The text says the uncertainty is substantial, which is right, but chart 04 plots the two periods as clean lines with no bands, and the most memorable sentence in the piece—“Maybe all the stresses … just bounced right off the 1%?”—rests on that interval. The defensible claim is the difference in changes: the gap against the rest of the top 10% widens by 13.62 points (2.06 to 25.18, p = 0.021). Put bands on the chart or plot the change; do not let the visual imply more precision than the estimate has.

  3. The wealth four-plus result is the thinnest number in the post. The post says “here it’s not just the third kid” and gives 19% versus 14% for four or more. The estimate is 18.97% versus 13.76%, a gap of 5.20 points, SE 2.47, 95% CI 0.37 to 10.04, p = 0.035. The direction is right and it is significant at the 5% level, but the lower bound is close to zero. The exactly-three wealth gap is much stronger: 7.74 points (0.93 to 14.55, p = 0.026). The four-plus result is doing real work in the “not just the third kid” sentence; present it with its interval or soften it to “somewhat more likely.”

Five easiest rebuttals

These are the objections I expect and the answers the evidence supports. They are not the three weaknesses above; they are the criticisms that look damaging at first but have a clean, already-estimated response.

  1. “This is not fertility. It is a household roster count that includes stepchildren, adopted children, children-in-law and double counting.” The post says this in the text and the methods. The estimand is a current-family child count at midlife, not births to a particular woman. For the claim “families with three or more children are more common at the top,” the family count is the right unit; a birth history would answer a different question. The specific counting defects are checked: maximum overlap correction, strict current first-marriage, and the older sample. The gap survives those checks; the exactly-three/four-plus decomposition shows where it sits. The count is imperfect, but it is not an arbitrary proxy for the thing being claimed.

  2. “Income and wealth are measured at 45–64, after the children are born. This is reverse causality or selection, not evidence that money causes births.” The post calls it a correlation and does not estimate a causal effect. The descriptive fact—top-1% families at midlife have more children—is still true. Childbearing can reduce earnings and wealth, especially for mothers, so reverse causality is at least as likely to work against the pattern as for it; it is not an obvious explanation for a positive top-1% gap. The mechanism and policy discussion is explicitly interpretation. The wealth ranking is measured later and is the stronger result, but it carries the same caveat.

  3. “The top 1% is defined within five-year age bands, not overall; the cutoff is arbitrary, and the dollar labels are approximate. The result is a ranking artifact.” The age-band ranking is deliberate: it compares families at the same stage of life, not 50-year-olds at peak earnings with 62-year-olds near retirement. Footnote 1 reruns the gap with ten-year bands, one 45–64 ranking, a whole-population ranking, a pooled five-survey ranking, and top-2%/top-0.5% cutoffs. All 15 reported gaps are positive with 95% intervals excluding zero. The top-1% bin holds 1.01% of the weight, so it is not a knife-edge boundary. The dollar labels are approximate; the ranks are not.

  4. “The SCF is a survey; the rich underreport or refuse; the public data are top-coded and perturbed; the top-1% cell is tiny, so a few families drive it.” The SCF’s list sample is drawn from IRS records specifically to oversample the wealthy, and the weights are designed to bring the list and area samples back to the population. The top-1% cell here has 1,390 distinct families across the five waves—1,322 in implicate 1, 198 to 313 per wave. That is not a handful. The standard errors use the Fed’s 999 replicate weights, re-estimate the top-1% cutoff in every replicate, and add imputation variance; the income gap’s 95% interval is 1.79 to 16.50 and the wealth gap’s is 5.50 to 20.39. The public-data edits are documented by the Fed. The X5910 top-code at 10 affects three families in the 45–64 early sample and cannot change a 3+ indicator. The Forbes 400 exclusion, if anything, makes the estimate conservative at the very top.

  5. “You didn’t control for education, marital status or race. The gap is just composition.” The headline is descriptive, so controls change the estimand; but the repository’s exploratory regressions with age and year plus race, education and marital status put the top-1% coefficient at 10.5 points (SE 4.0) for 3+ children, and 10.5 points (SE 4.8) among married couples. The 90th–99th coefficient in the same specification is 1.8 points (SE 3.3). Controls do not explain the top-1% advantage. These are approximate clustered standard errors, not the design-based headline tests, but the direction is clear.
