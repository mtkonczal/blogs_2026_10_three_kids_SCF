Three Weakest Points

  1. The family child count is an imperfect measure of childbearing. It combines children associated with either partner, including stepchildren and some children-in-law. Marriage-history restrictions and overlap corrections address specific problems, but cannot establish that other measurement errors are unrelated to wealth. Some of the pattern could reflect differences in family structure rather than births. The post discloses this limitation; the public SCF cannot fully resolve it.

  2. The exactly-three category does not measure a transition to a third birth. The income gap is concentrated in families counted as having exactly three children. That does not establish that wealthy two-child families are especially likely to have another child. Conditioning on a count of at least two gives an income comparison of 57.6% versus 51.0%, with a 6.6-point gap and 95% interval of −1.4 to 14.5. The wealth comparison is significant. This limits the behavioral interpretation of the income result, while the main 49%-versus-40% comparison is supported.

  3. The financial-insulation mechanism is not identified. Income and wealth are measured largely after childbearing. Selection, inheritance, partnership patterns and effects of children on earnings can influence the relationship. The historical top-income group’s estimated change has an interval spanning roughly a 10-point decline to an 11-point increase. The widening relative gap is supported, but an unchanged top-group share or insulation from childbearing pressures cannot be established. The policy argument is an interpretation of the pattern rather than an estimated treatment effect.

5 Easiest Rebuttals

  1. “You are just finding that rich parents still have children at home, while poorer parents’ children have moved out.”

    The measure includes living children who have moved out. It adds the household roster count to X5910, the survey question about sons and daughters of either partner living elsewhere, including children from previous marriages and adopted children. It remains an imperfect family child count rather than an individual birth history; the post discloses possible overlap and resident children-in-law and tests specific counting corrections.

  2. “This is just rich people remarrying and adding their spouses’ children together.”

    That cannot fully explain the reported comparison. Among currently married couples in which both spouses report a first marriage, the gap remains positive and statistically significant using the original full-population income and wealth ranking universe, including after the specified duplicate-count correction. This does not eliminate every blended-family concern. Re-ranking only within first-marriage couples answers a different question and gives a smaller, imprecise wealth gap.

  3. “The SCF oversamples rich people, so of course it makes big rich families look more common.”

    The estimates use survey weights, not raw sample proportions. The top percentile represents approximately 1% of its weighted ranking population, despite containing far more than 1% of sample observations. Oversampling wealthy families does not mechanically create a higher child-count share within that group. The uncertainty calculations use the Fed’s replicate weights and account for multiple imputation.

  4. “You manufactured the result by choosing a convenient definition of the top 1%.”

    The main ranking compares families within five-year age bands and survey years. The positive gap also appears with broader age groups, national rankings, pooled-year rankings, alternative top-group cutoffs, and actual rather than usual income for the income comparison. Across the reported definitions, the gaps run about 7–15 percentage points by income and 9–16 by wealth. These are overlapping sensitivity checks, not independent replications or evidence that every possible sample restriction preserves the result.

  5. “You have not proved that money causes a third child, so there is no finding here.”

    The finding is descriptive: three-plus family child counts are more common in the top percentile than in the rest of the top decile under the stated definitions. The analysis does not identify a causal effect of money on births or observe transitions from a second child to a third. Affordability is a proposed explanation, and the policy conclusion is an argument informed by the pattern rather than an estimated treatment effect.

Executive summary

All 26 checkable claims in the draft are true as written, and none is false. Every number that appears in the text or on a chart reproduces from the raw Federal Reserve files. The three citations resolve and say what the post says they say.

I did not take the production scripts’ word for anything. The methodology is better than the post lets on. Wording

No sentence in the draft is wrong as written. Four smaller things are still open, three of them one-clause fixes.

1. The post never says the waves are weighted equally. The pooled estimates give each of the five surveys equal total weight rather than weighting by the number of families it represents. The chart captions say so; the text says only that it pools the last five surveys. With population growth of about 10% across the twelve years the two schemes barely differ here, so this is disclosure rather than a problem. “I pool the last five surveys, weighting each equally” closes it.

2. “Compared with the 1990s” describes a period that begins in 1989. The early period is the 1989, 1992, 1995 and 1998 waves, and the chart labels that series 1989–1998. The text should use the same span the figure does.

3. “Also more likely to have four or more (19% versus 14%)” is the thinnest number in the post. True at p = 0.035, with a 95% interval reaching down to 0.4 points. I graded it true and it is defensible, but it is the one headline figure a hostile reader can push on, and it sits in the sentence more prominently than its margin supports.

4. Footnote 1 lists one alternative that exists only on the income side. The footnote says the gap holds “for both income and wealth” and then ends its list with “or use actual rather than usual income”, which cannot change a wealth ranking. Nothing is wrong, but a careful reader counting cells comes up one short. Details under Footnote 1 and footnote 2.

One thing the post could add rather than fix. The bottom income decile sits at 36.0%, below the 80th–90th dip the text singles out as the low point, and the wealth chart has the opposite shape down there. That contrast is interesting in its own right and would be worth a sentence if the piece has room.

Two places where I expected to find a problem and did not are worth recording. The claim that the average child count is noisy because a few very large families move it is the kind of mechanism writers assert without testing; I tested it, and capping the child count at four cuts the standard error of the income gap from 0.095 to 0.082 and raises the t-statistic from 1.66 to 2.09, so the tail is in fact where the imprecision lives. Requiring both partners to report a current marriage and a first marriage, 70.1% of all top-1% families qualify, against 63.6% in the rest of the top 10%. On the looser flag that also admits cohabiters the shares are 71.2% and 64.3%, and among partnered families alone 77.5% and 69.8%. Every version runs the same way.

Method: the child count

This is the measure the whole post rests on, so I checked it against the codebook term by term.

KIDS comes from the Board’s own macro, which counts roster slots X108 through X226 holding relationship code 4 (child, including in-law), 13, or 36 (foster child, suppressed in the public file). There is no age restriction, so adult children living at home count, and there are ten roster slots, so the count is capped at ten. The post’s description, that the Fed’s summary data counts only children listed in the household and that resident children-in-law can slip in, is exactly what the macro does.

X5910 asks how many sons and daughters the respondent or their spouse or partner have who do not live with them, explicitly including children from previous marriages and adopted children, and instructing that only living children and step-children be included. Value −1 means none. The code uses pmax(x5910, 0), which is the right recode, and I confirmed from the raw files that no wave contains a value below −1 and none is missing, so nothing else is being swept into zero.

The double-count correction in R/29_family_count_checks.R is implemented correctly, which is the part I most expected to find wrong. It pairs each relationship item (X108, X114, …, X226) with the corresponding “does this person usually live with you” item (X112, X118, …, X230); I checked the pairing against the codebook and the two ten-element arrays line up slot for slot, persons three through twelve. A roster child coded 5 on the residence item is a candidate for being reported again in X5910, and the script subtracts min(away_roster, kids_away), the largest overlap arithmetically possible. The marital restriction is also right: X8023 = 1 and X105 = 1 require both members to be legally married rather than cohabiting, which matters because the Fed’s MARRIED variable lumps partners in with spouses, and X7377 = 1 and X7392 = 1 require both to report a first marriage. X19 is the spouse’s reconciled age, so the 50–64 check with a partner aged at least 45 does what footnote 2 says.

Two top-coding details worth knowing. X5910 is top-coded at 10 in the public data from 1995 onward but not before: across the full public files it reaches 16 in 1989 and 15 in 1992. That makes the early period’s mean child count slightly less comparable with the recent period’s. It does not touch the historical chart, which plots the share with three or more, where anything above three is already above three. The second: the Board’s macro notes that household listing information was collected for one fewer member from 1995 forward, another small early-versus-late asymmetry in the same direction. Neither affects the 2013–2025 results at all.

The honest summary, which the post gives, is that this is a family child count and not a birth history. The two sources are of comparable size in the top bands: among top-1% families by income the mean roster count is 1.14 and the mean away count is 1.37, and 59.9% report at least one child living elsewhere.

Method: sample, ranks and weights

The sample is families whose reference person is 45 to 64, in the 2013, 2016, 2019, 2022 and 2025 waves: 11,215 families, 56,075 family-implicate rows. The post calls this mid-life, which is the right word; the SCF reference person is the male in a mixed-sex couple and the older member of a same-sex couple, so in practice the age band is the husband’s age and wives are typically a couple of years younger. I verified the rule is stable across the whole history, with the female share of married reference persons between 0% and 2.9% in every wave, so nothing shifts under the series. Footnote 2’s 50–64-with-partner-45-plus restriction is the right check for this and strengthens the result rather than weakening it.

Ranks are weighted mid-ranks, (cumulative weight − own weight / 2) / total weight, computed within survey year, implicate and five-year age band. The implementation is correct and I reproduced it. The band edges are right-closed, so the top 1% is rank > 0.99 in every script that touches it, and R/28 adds a pmin(rank, 1) guard for replicate draws where zero weights at the top can push a cumulative share a hair above one. The sanity check that matters is whether the bands hold the weight they claim to: recomputed, the top 1% holds 1.01% of the weight, the 90th–99th holds 9.01% and the 40th–60th holds 20.02%.

Pooling gives each wave equal total weight rather than weighting by the number of families it represents. That is a defensible choice and the charts say so in their captions, but the post itself only says it pools the last five surveys. With population growth of about 10% across the twelve years the two schemes barely differ here, so this is a disclosure point rather than a problem.

One implementation note for the author rather than the reader. output/kids_main.rds contains a column named y, left over from the generic outcome assignment in R/07. Inside a data.table filter, a loop variable called y is then masked by that column, silently returning nothing. The production scripts all dodge this by naming their loop variables yr and wv, which I assume is deliberate, but it is a live trap for anyone who writes a new script against that file. Dropping the y column before saving would remove it.

Method: imputation and replicate weights

The SCF gives five implicates per family and 999 bootstrap replicate weights for implicate 1. The recipe used here is the standard one: sampling variance from the 999 replicates with denominator 998, plus (1 + 1/5) = 1.2 times the between-implicate variance of the statistic. Headline point estimates average the five implicate-specific ratios rather than pooling every implicate row into a single ratio; for subgroup shares those are not algebraically identical, though here they differ by at most 0.004 points. I confirmed the 1.2 factor, the 998 divisor, and that replicate weights are built as wt1b{r} × mm{r} with missing set to zero, which is the Board’s construction.

Three things here are done better than the minimum and are worth defending if anyone asks.

First, ranks are re-estimated inside every replicate. “Top 1%” is itself an estimated cutoff, and holding the cutoff fixed while resampling understates the uncertainty. R/25, R/28 and R/29 all recompute the within-age-band percentile under each replicate weight before assigning bands. Here that raises the headline gap’s standard error from 3.53 to 3.75 points by income and from 3.60 to 3.80 by wealth, which is the direction a reader should want. It is not a general law: carrying an estimated cutoff through a statistic need not always inflate its variance.

Second, the five waves are treated as independent cross-sections and their replicate variances summed, rather than pretending replicate r in 2013 and replicate r in 2025 are the same draw. They are not, and the code does not assume they are.

Third, the pooled share is a ratio of summed moments, so its variance is obtained through the Jacobian of that ratio by central differences. I was suspicious of the linearization, so I replaced it with the exact calculation: for each wave in turn, substitute that wave’s replicate moments into the pooled statistic, hold the other four waves at their point values, take the variance across the 999 draws, and sum over waves. The two approaches agree to better than half a percent on every statistic. The delta method is fine here.

The honest limitation, which the repository documents, is that the within-imputation variance comes from implicate 1 alone, because the Board only publishes replicate weights for implicate 1. Rubin’s rule wants the average across implicates. This is the universal SCF compromise rather than a choice made here, and the between-implicate term is added on top, so the direction of any error is small. Intervals are pointwise normal approximations and are not adjusted for the number of comparisons; see the last section.

Three weakest points

  1. The income version of the third-child story does not survive conditioning on being a parent. The post opens on a behavioral claim, that a third child is what distinguishes the 1% from the merely affluent. The supporting statistic compares the share with three or more among all families, which mixes together the decision to have a third child and the decision to have any children at all. Those are different, and the top 1% differs on both: it has fewer childless families (7.5% against 9.4%) and far fewer one-child families (7.2% against 12.3%). I recomputed the conditional version with my own estimator. Among families with at least two children, the share with three or more is 57.59% in the top 1% against 51.02% in the rest of the top 10%: a gap of 6.57 points, SE 4.07, 95% CI −1.41 to 14.55, p = 0.107. By wealth it survives, at 11.52 points, p = 0.002. So the sentence the piece is named after is the one that weakens first, and it weakens on the ranking the piece leads with.

  2. The historical paragraph is the most load-bearing and the least precisely estimated, and its chart shows no uncertainty. “Maybe all the stresses that made childbearing more difficult over the past 30 years just bounced right off the 1%” is the most memorable line in the post, and it rests on a top-1% change of +0.62 points with a standard error of 5.26, an interval running from a ten-point fall to an eleven-point rise. The text hedges this properly. The chart does not: chart 04 plots the 1989–1998 line as a clean series with no bands, and its visible features are mostly noise, including a spike to 57% at the 60th–70th percentile that no reader should take seriously. The defensible claim is the difference in changes, where the gap against the rest of the top 10% widens by 13.62 points, SE 5.90, p = 0.021. I would put bands on that chart, or plot the change rather than the two levels.

  3. The measure is a family child count, the ranking is at interview age, and the post asks the reader to hold both of those in mind at once. The counting checks in footnote 2 are real and they pass, but they correct arithmetic overlap and marital history; they cannot turn a roster-plus-elsewhere count into a birth history, and the audit directory honestly retains specifications where stripping all adult roster children from the top group alone sends both intervals through zero. At the same time, income and wealth are measured at ages 45 to 64, fifteen to thirty years after the births in question, so the ranking is partly an outcome of the fertility it is being used to explain, through inheritance, assortative matching, and the effect of children on earnings. The post says “this is a correlation”, which is the right disclaimer. It does not say that the top 1% by income here is a group with a median usual income around $2.1 million, assembled from roughly 200 to 310 unweighted families per wave in implicate 1, and ranked on a measure that admits capital income. Usual income blunts that, since the Fed substitutes a reported normal-year figure when the previous year was unusual, so I cannot show that one-off gains determine membership. The wealth ranking is less transitory and gives the larger and more significant gap. On the evidence I checked, wealth is the stronger result and deserves to lead.

Five easiest rebuttals

These are the five objections most likely to arrive, and the five where the answer was already computed before publication. They are deliberately not the three points above: an objection that lands on one of those should be conceded, not argued. Every number here was recomputed on this machine.

  1. “You are double-counting. A kid away at college sits on the household roster and in the away-children question.”

    Real, named in the post, and already corrected. The check pairs each of the ten roster slots with its matching “does this person usually live with you” item, slot for slot, a pairing I verified against the 2025 codebook, and then subtracts min(roster children away, children reported elsewhere). That is the largest overlap arithmetically possible, not an estimate of the likely one. The gap survives it. So does the deliberately adverse version, which applies the correction only to the top 1% and leaves the comparison group untouched: 7.84 points by income (p = 0.038) and 12.26 by wealth (p = 0.002). To break the result this way an objector has to argue the true overlap exceeds its own arithmetic maximum.

  2. “It is remarriage. Wealthy men with second families accumulate two sets of children.”

    The composition runs the other way. Requiring both partners to report a current marriage and a first marriage, 70.1% of all top-1% families qualify against 63.6% in the rest of the top 10%; among partnered families alone it is 77.5% against 69.8%. The top 1% is more first-married, not less. The restriction itself also passes: combined with the duplicate-overlap correction it leaves 11.84 points by income (p = 0.014) and 10.65 by wealth (p = 0.032). What it does not do is exclude every kind of blended family, and footnote 2 says so. Concede that clause and keep the rest.

  3. “A survey cannot measure the top 1%. You have maybe a hundred families up there.”

    This one is simply wrong about the SCF, which is why it is the most confidently answerable. The Fed draws a list sample from statistical records derived from tax data precisely to populate the top of the distribution. The top-1% cell here holds 1,322 unweighted families in implicate 1, between 198 and 313 per wave, and carries 1.01% of the survey weight, which is both the right answer and a check that the ranking works. Inference runs on all 999 bootstrap replicate weights, and the percentile cutoff is re-estimated inside every one of them, so the uncertainty about who is in the top 1% is carried through rather than assumed away. That raises the standard error, from 3.53 to 3.75 points by income and 3.60 to 3.80 by wealth, and the gap clears significance anyway.

  4. “You picked the cutoff that worked.”

    Eight alternative definitions were run and all eight are reported. Income gaps: 7.42, 8.80, 9.14, 10.61, 11.51, 12.00, 13.77, 14.64. Wealth gaps: 9.27, 10.77, 12.51, 12.94, 13.41, 14.20, 16.14. Every one is positive and significant, and the largest p-value across all fifteen contrasts is 0.017. The published income figure, 9.14, is the third smallest of its eight; the published wealth figure, 12.94, sits at the median of its seven. If this specification was chosen to flatter, it was chosen badly. The alternatives include ten-year age bands, ranking all 45-to-64 families together, ranking against the whole population, one pooled ranking across waves, actual instead of usual income, the top 2% against the 90th–98th, and the top 0.5% against the 90th–99.5th.

  5. “The top 1% by income is just whoever had one good year. Somebody sold a business.”

    Partly blocked by construction, and settled by the wealth ranking. The income rank uses usual income, where the Fed substitutes a respondent-reported normal-year figure when the previous year was unusual, so a single realization does not by itself buy membership. I could not show that one-off gains drive the top band; neither can the objector. The decisive answer is that the same pattern appears, larger and more precisely estimated, when families are ranked by net worth, which is a stock and far less transitory: 12.94 points (p = 0.0007) against 9.14 by income (p = 0.015). Footnote 1 adds that it holds on actual rather than usual income too. Pressed hard, this objection is an argument for leading with wealth, which is what I recommend above.

The common thread is that each of these five was anticipated and tested before the post went out, so the answer is a number rather than a posture. The three weakest points above were not solved, and the piece is better served by conceding them than by defending them.

Methodology audit

I rebuilt the main analysis from the raw files rather than reading the production objects: 13 summary CSVs, 13 full Stata files, and 9 replicate-weight files. I used the saved production objects only for the published comparison and one period-change diagnostic. The rebuild reproduces the production sample, child counts, ranks, headline contrasts, standard errors and all 24 plotted bin values. I also ran checks the production scripts do not run: population-weighted waves, average ranks for ties, a re-ranked first-marriage subsample, an adult-roster adverse count, and a capped mean.

Data construction and joins

The raw archives were already present; I did not re-download them. This verifies the local October 2026 vintage, not a fresh network pull. The manifest records the archive MD5s and sizes.

The 13 waves are clean one-to-one joins. In every wave, the summary extract and the full public file have the same number of rows, Y1 is unique in both, every family appears in all five implicates, and the implicate index Y1 − 10×YY1 takes values 1–5 with equal rows per implicate. The summary weight identity holds record by record: the maximum relative error in 5×WGT = X42001 is 1.4×10−10 in 1989 and about 5×10−11 in every later wave. Reconstructing KIDS from the roster relationship codes gives zero disagreements in all 13 waves.

The 2013–2025 analysis sample is 11,215 families and 56,075 family–implicate rows. The top 1% by income contains 1,390 distinct year–family cases (12.4% of the unweighted sample, reflecting the oversample), and 208 in the 2025 wave alone. In any one wave and implicate, the top-1% bin holds 1.01% of the weight, the 90th–99th holds about 9.0%, and the 40th–60th holds about 20.0%, which is the check that the weighted ranks are doing what they claim.

The production merge in R/07_kids_income.R stops if the joined row count differs from the extract. That is sufficient for these files because (year, Y1) is unique in both; it is not a general key check. I verified uniqueness and exact ID-set equality independently. Adding stopifnot(!anyDuplicated(...)) would make the check self-documenting for a future vintage.

Dollar units are handled correctly: the summary extract is in 2025 dollars, the full public data are nominal, and every post comparison uses the extract. The only value-level edit in R/02 is blanking the usual-income categories in 1989 and 1992, where NORMINC was not collected; the analysis does not use those categories.

The child count

The formula is kids = KIDS + max(X5910, 0). KIDS counts roster relationship codes 4 (child, including in-law), 13 and 36 (foster child, suppressed in the public file). There is no age restriction, so adult children living at home count, and there are at most ten roster slots. X5910 is the “children living elsewhere” question; the 2025 wording is exactly what the post quotes, −1 means none, and there are no missing values or values below −1 in any wave.

The public files are top-coded at 10 from 1995 onward. In 1989 the full file reaches 16 and in 1992 it reaches 15. The methods says “three families in the 1989 and 1992 files report more.” That is true only in the 45–64 analysis sample: two families in 1989 and one in 1992. The full public files contain 40 families above 10 in 1989 and 50 in 1992. The sentence should say “in the age-eligible sample.” The practical effect is small: removing the cap changes the early 45–64 mean away-count by 0.006 children in 1989 and 0.0007 in 1992, and cannot change a 3+ indicator.

The double-count correction is implemented correctly. The roster-away count pairs relationship items X108…X226 with residence items X112…X230, persons three through twelve; a child coded 4/13/36 with residence 5 is a candidate for being reported again in X5910. Subtracting min(A, B) is the largest overlap arithmetically possible under that flag. The strict first-marriage restriction correctly requires both current-marital responses to be “married” (X8023 = 1, X105 = 1) and both first-marriage responses to be 1 (X7377 = 1, X7392 = 1), which excludes cohabiters. The older-sample check uses X19; substituting X104 gives identical results in this sample.

Two limitations the post states are real and cannot be fixed with these data: KIDS can include resident children-in-law and adult children, and X5910 includes stepchildren and children from previous marriages. The count is a current-family count, not a birth history. There are also missing final-slot roster fields in 1995–2004: only nine of the ten relationship slots exist, and the methods’ “all ten roster positions” is therefore slightly imprecise for those waves. The summary KIDS still matches the available slots exactly in every wave.

Sample, ranks and weights

The sample is the reference person aged 45–64. In a mixed-sex couple the reference person is the man, so a younger partner may still be fecund; the older-sample check addresses that. Ranks are weighted mid-ranks, (cumulative weight − own weight/2) / total weight, computed within survey year, implicate and five-year age band. The bin edges are right-closed, so the top 1% is rank > 0.99, consistently in R/07, R/25, R/28 and R/29. The 12 bins are the nine lower deciles plus 90–95, 95–99 and top 1%.

Each wave gets equal total weight (WGT / sum(WGT) within year and implicate). I tested the alternative: weighting waves by their population. The income gap moves from 9.14 to 9.08 points and the wealth gap from 12.94 to 13.01. The choice is immaterial here, but the post does not say which it uses; the methods and chart captions do.

Equal financial values are split by record order in the production rank. I tested the alternative of assigning the average rank to tied values. For wealth, where zero and negative net worth create large ties, the largest change in any bin’s 3+ share is under 0.06 percentage points, and the top-1% result is unchanged. Tie handling is not a source of the result.

Income is NORMINC, the Fed’s usual-income measure, which includes capital income and adds pension-account withdrawals. In 1989 and 1992 the usual-income amount was not collected, so those waves use actual INCOME; the historical section discloses this and runs an all-actual-income sensitivity. There are no missing NORMINC values in 1995–2025. Neither income nor net worth is equivalized for family size; the post compares families at the same age and rank, not families with the same per-person resources. That is a real limitation of the “money solves the hard parts” reading, and the methods states it.

Inference

The standard-error recipe is the Fed’s: 999 replicate weights for implicate 1, effective weight wt1b{r} × mm{r} with missing products set to zero, sampling variance with denominator 998, and imputation variance (1 + 1/5)B added. This matches the codebook’s MEANIT and MISECOMP formulas. Ranks are re-estimated inside every replicate, so the uncertainty in who is in the top 1% is carried through; that raises the income 3+ gap SE from about 3.55 to 3.75 and the wealth gap SE from about 3.61 to 3.80. The five waves are treated as independent cross-sections and their replicate variances are summed through the gradient of the pooled ratio; the top group and its comparison group are therefore allowed to covary.

I rebuilt the headline statistics with an independently written estimator and the same Fed inputs. The point estimates match production to 10−6; the standard errors match to better than 0.001 (income gap 3.7531 both; wealth gap 3.8007 both). The 24 charted bin values also reproduce. Two implementation details are worth recording. First, the production gradient is evaluated at implicate 1’s pooled moments rather than the five-implicate mean; using the mean changes the income gap SE from 3.7531 to 3.7545, which is immaterial. Second, the period-change test computes imputation variance on the paired recent-minus-early contrast; using the sum of the two periods’ imputation variances instead gives a slightly larger SE (about 0.05 points on the gap change), not a different conclusion.

The honest limitation is the one the methods states: the within-imputation sampling variance comes from implicate 1 alone because the Fed publishes replicate weights only for implicate 1. That is the standard SCF compromise, not a choice specific to this project. The intervals are pointwise normal approximations and are not adjusted for the number of outcomes and sensitivity definitions. The income headline p = 0.015 would not survive a strict correction across all the outcomes examined; the wealth headline p = 0.0007 is much more secure. That is another reason the wealth result deserves to lead.

Three weakest points

None of these is an arithmetic error. They are the places where the evidence is thinner than the prose, or where the post leaves out a choice that changes how a number should be read.

  1. The income “third kid” story weakens when you condition on already having two children. The headline compares the 3+ share among all families. That mixes the decision to have any children with the decision to have a third, and the top 1% differs on both margins. Among families with at least two children, the income top 1% has a 3+ share of 57.59% against 51.02% in the rest of the top 10%: a gap of 6.57 points, SE 4.07, 95% CI −1.41 to 14.55, p = 0.106. The wealth version survives: 60.46% versus 48.94%, gap 11.52 points, 95% CI 4.04 to 18.99. The descriptive 49-versus-40 comparison is correct, and the exactly-three decomposition is correct; what does not follow is that the income top 1% is specifically more likely to have a third child rather than more likely to have children at all. This is the single strongest reason to lead with wealth rather than income.

  2. The historical paragraph is a non-detection shown on an uncertainty-free chart. The top-1% share goes from 48.51% in 1989–1998 to 49.13% in 2013–2025: a change of +0.62 points with a standard error of 5.26 and a 95% interval of −9.69 to +10.93. The text says the uncertainty is substantial, which is right, but chart 04 plots the two periods as clean lines with no bands, and the most memorable sentence in the piece—“Maybe all the stresses … just bounced right off the 1%?”—rests on that interval. The defensible claim is the difference in changes: the gap against the rest of the top 10% widens by 13.62 points (2.06 to 25.18, p = 0.021). Put bands on the chart or plot the change; do not let the visual imply more precision than the estimate has.

  3. The wealth four-plus result is the thinnest number in the post. The post says “here it’s not just the third kid” and gives 19% versus 14% for four or more. The estimate is 18.97% versus 13.76%, a gap of 5.20 points, SE 2.47, 95% CI 0.37 to 10.04, p = 0.035. The direction is right and it is significant at the 5% level, but the lower bound is close to zero. The exactly-three wealth gap is much stronger: 7.74 points (0.93 to 14.55, p = 0.026). The four-plus result is doing real work in the “not just the third kid” sentence; present it with its interval or soften it to “somewhat more likely.”

Five easiest rebuttals

These are the objections I expect and the answers the evidence supports. They are not the three weaknesses above; they are the criticisms that look damaging at first but have a clean, already-estimated response.

  1. “This is not fertility. It is a household roster count that includes stepchildren, adopted children, children-in-law and double counting.” The post says this in the text and the methods. The estimand is a current-family child count at midlife, not births to a particular woman. For the claim “families with three or more children are more common at the top,” the family count is the right unit; a birth history would answer a different question. The specific counting defects are checked: maximum overlap correction, strict current first-marriage, and the older sample. The gap survives those checks; the exactly-three/four-plus decomposition shows where it sits. The count is imperfect, but it is not an arbitrary proxy for the thing being claimed.

  2. “Income and wealth are measured at 45–64, after the children are born. This is reverse causality or selection, not evidence that money causes births.” The post calls it a correlation and does not estimate a causal effect. The descriptive fact—top-1% families at midlife have more children—is still true. Childbearing can reduce earnings and wealth, especially for mothers, so reverse causality is at least as likely to work against the pattern as for it; it is not an obvious explanation for a positive top-1% gap. The mechanism and policy discussion is explicitly interpretation. The wealth ranking is measured later and is the stronger result, but it carries the same caveat.

  3. “The top 1% is defined within five-year age bands, not overall; the cutoff is arbitrary, and the dollar labels are approximate. The result is a ranking artifact.” The age-band ranking is deliberate: it compares families at the same stage of life, not 50-year-olds at peak earnings with 62-year-olds near retirement. Footnote 1 reruns the gap with ten-year bands, one 45–64 ranking, a whole-population ranking, a pooled five-survey ranking, and top-2%/top-0.5% cutoffs. All 15 reported gaps are positive with 95% intervals excluding zero. The top-1% bin holds 1.01% of the weight, so it is not a knife-edge boundary. The dollar labels are approximate; the ranks are not.

  4. “The SCF is a survey; the rich underreport or refuse; the public data are top-coded and perturbed; the top-1% cell is tiny, so a few families drive it.” The SCF’s list sample is drawn from IRS records specifically to oversample the wealthy, and the weights are designed to bring the list and area samples back to the population. The top-1% cell here has 1,390 distinct families across the five waves—1,322 in implicate 1, 198 to 313 per wave. That is not a handful. The standard errors use the Fed’s 999 replicate weights, re-estimate the top-1% cutoff in every replicate, and add imputation variance; the income gap’s 95% interval is 1.79 to 16.50 and the wealth gap’s is 5.50 to 20.39. The public-data edits are documented by the Fed. The X5910 top-code at 10 affects three families in the 45–64 early sample and cannot change a 3+ indicator. The Forbes 400 exclusion, if anything, makes the estimate conservative at the very top.

  5. “You didn’t control for education, marital status or race. The gap is just composition.” The headline is descriptive, so controls change the estimand; but the repository’s exploratory regressions with age and year plus race, education and marital status put the top-1% coefficient at 10.5 points (SE 4.0) for 3+ children, and 10.5 points (SE 4.8) among married couples. The 90th–99th coefficient in the same specification is 1.8 points (SE 3.3). Controls do not explain the top-1% advantage. These are approximate clustered standard errors, not the design-based headline tests, but the direction is clear.

Capping counts: the numbers are right; the conclusion is too strong

Claude’s calculation reproduces. For income, the ordinary mean gap is 0.1583 children, SE 0.09527, p = 0.0967. Replacing the count with min(count, 4) gives 0.1722, SE 0.08237, p = 0.0365; t rises from 1.66 to 2.09. The standard error falls about 14%.

This is useful evidence that the upper tail contributes to uncertainty. It changes the estimated gap as well as its standard error, so crossing the 5% threshold cannot be attributed entirely to reduced noise. The ordinary count equals its capped value plus the excess above four; the variance of its gap also includes their covariance. In the income comparison, the excess-count gap is −0.0140 (SE 0.02841), and its covariance with the capped gap is positive. Families above four account for about 5.35% of the top group’s weighted population and 6.35% of P90–99. My earlier review should now acknowledge this new evidence. The original claim was untested in the headline results alone; the new check supports the narrower wording: “The income mean gap is imprecisely estimated, and its uncertainty is sensitive to larger child counts.”
